Couples Therapy in Famagusta: When, Why and How to Start

Couples therapy is valuable not only in moments of crisis, but for any couple who wants their relationship to stand on healthier ground. Starting at the right time is less about saving a relationship and more about strengthening it.

Research shows that couples wait an average of six years before seeking professional help. That delay typically pushes the relationship to a more difficult place. Seeking support early shortens the process and improves outcomes.

When should a couple consider therapy?

If one or more of the following signs are present, couples therapy is worth considering:

The same arguments keep recurring

When disputes that start over different topics consistently hit the same wall, it points to a deep-rooted pattern underneath. A psychologist helps make those cycles visible.

Communication has broken down or become frozen

"We talk but get nowhere" or "I've stopped wanting to talk" — these feelings signal that communication patterns need restructuring, which is precisely what couples therapy addresses.

A breach of trust or infidelity

If you want to keep the relationship going after a betrayal, couples therapy can help manage that process in a structured and safe way. Trust can be rebuilt — but it takes time and guidance.

Major life transitions

Having a child, moving home, a change of job, bereavement — big transitions put strain on a couple's alignment. Therapy makes it easier to find shared ground during these periods.

Growing distance

An increase in physical or emotional distance, a sense of drifting apart from each other — these are signs that the relationship needs to reconnect.

Couples therapy is not a place people go to end a relationship

This is one of the most common misconceptions about couples therapy. The psychologist does not take sides or decide who is right. The goal is for both partners to understand each other better, improve communication quality, and change the patterns within the relationship. Sometimes the process does end with a decision to separate — but that decision belongs to the couple, not to the psychologist.

How does couples therapy work?

In the first session the psychologist listens to each partner — either separately or together — to understand the relationship's history and the current issues. Shared goals are then agreed upon.

Sessions typically last 50–60 minutes. Through active listening, reframing and communication exercises, the psychologist provides practical tools that the couple can use between sessions.

The process generally runs for 8–16 sessions, though this can be shorter or longer depending on the couple's dynamics and goals.

"My partner won't agree to therapy" — what can you do?

This is a very common situation. A partner's resistance usually stems from fear: "I'll be blamed", "I'll look weak", or "This won't work." These feelings are normal.

What you can do: share your reasons for wanting to go without placing blame. Try framing it as "I want to feel better in this relationship" rather than "you need to change". Point out that the first session is purely introductory — no decisions are made in the first meeting.

If your partner still does not want to attend, starting individual therapy is a valid option. Your own change will transform the relationship dynamics too.

Individual therapy versus couples therapy: what is the difference?

In individual therapy the focus is on the person's inner world, history and individual patterns. In couples therapy the focus shifts to the relationship system between two people. The psychologist holds both partners' perspectives at the same time.

In some cases individual therapy is recommended alongside couples therapy — particularly when one partner needs to address a personal issue such as trauma, anxiety or attachment patterns.
