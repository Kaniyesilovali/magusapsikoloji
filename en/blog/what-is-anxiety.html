What is Anxiety? Symptoms and How to Recognise It in Yourself

Constant tension, thoughts that won't let you sleep, a nameless dread you can't quite explain — these could all be signs of anxiety.

What is anxiety?

The word "anxiety" comes from the Latin anxietas — meaning to be squeezed or choked. Medically, anxiety is an intense state of worry, fear or tension in response to a real or perceived threat.

Some anxiety is completely normal. Nerves before an exam, excitement ahead of an important interview, apprehension when making a risky decision — these are all healthy, functional forms of anxiety. They motivate us and keep us safe.

The problem arises when anxiety appears without a genuine threat, persists over time, or begins to disrupt everyday life.

What are the symptoms of anxiety?

Anxiety symptoms fall into three groups: physical, emotional and behavioural.

Physical

  • • Palpitations, racing heart
  • • Sweating, trembling
  • • Shortness of breath, choking feeling
  • • Nausea, digestive issues
  • • Muscle tension, headaches
  • • Sleep difficulties, chronic fatigue

Emotional

  • • Persistent sense of dread
  • • Feeling something will go wrong
  • • Irritability and tension
  • • Fear of losing control
  • • Difficulty concentrating
  • • Mind going blank

Behavioural

  • • Avoiding triggers
  • • Excessive checking or planning
  • • Seeking reassurance
  • • Withdrawing socially
  • • Procrastinating on anxious tasks
  • • Over-relying on distractions

How common is it?

Approximately 1 in 4 people will experience an anxiety disorder at some point in their lives, making it the most common mental health condition globally. (WHO)

Normal anxiety vs an anxiety disorder

The line can be hard to draw. The following factors suggest anxiety may have crossed into a clinical disorder:

  • Duration: longer than six weeks

    If worry persists even after the stressor has passed, that is a significant signal.

  • Disproportionate intensity

    When the level of worry is out of proportion to the actual threat and is hard to control.

  • Disrupted daily functioning

    Work, relationships, sleep or social life are being negatively affected.

  • Accompanying physical symptoms

    Persistent palpitations, sweating or shortness of breath that don't have another medical cause.

Do you have anxiety? Five questions to ask yourself

These questions are not a clinical diagnosis. They are a starting point to help you decide whether professional support might be useful.

1

Have you felt anxious or on edge almost every day for the past six weeks?

2

Is it becoming difficult to stop or control the worry?

3

Has your sleep been disrupted, or do you feel constantly tired?

4

Is this affecting your work, relationships or social life?

5

Are you experiencing physical symptoms such as palpitations, sweating or shortness of breath?

If you answered yes to three or more, a psychological assessment could be a valuable next step.

Can anxiety be treated?

Yes — with the right support, anxiety is highly manageable and often resolves fully. The main evidence-based approaches are:

  • Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (CBT)

    Focuses on changing the thought patterns and avoidance behaviours that fuel anxiety. CBT has the strongest evidence base for anxiety treatment.

  • EMDR

    Particularly effective for anxiety rooted in past traumatic experiences.

  • Mindfulness-based approaches

    Anchoring attention to the present moment helps break the anxiety cycle and build body awareness.

When should you seek help?

If anxiety symptoms have lasted more than six weeks, are disrupting your daily functioning, or have led to avoidance behaviours, speaking with a psychologist is recommended. Early support makes treatment more straightforward.
